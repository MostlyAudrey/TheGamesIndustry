# Nate Nanzer — Former Overwatch League Commissioner, Epic Games

**The executive who tried to make esports a proper professional sport.** As the first Overwatch League Commissioner, Nate Nanzer designed the most ambitious esports league structure ever attempted. He later joined Epic Games to lead competitive Fortnite.

## Career arc

- **Pre-Blip/Early career:** Background in traditional sports, media, and esports. Worked at a sports agency and an esports organization before joining Blizzard.
- **2016:** Joined Activision Blizzard to help build the Overwatch League — a city-based franchised esports league inspired by traditional professional sports.
- **2018 (January):** Named first Overwatch League Commissioner. Managed the league's first season, which was broadcast on ESPN, Disney, and ABC.
- **2019 (October):** Left Blizzard — a surprise departure. Joined Epic Games to lead competitive Fortnite.
- **2019–2024:** At Epic, Nanzer oversaw the Fortnite Champion Series (FNCS), the Fortnite World Cup, and Epic's broader competitive gaming strategy.
- **2024–2026:** Epic restructured its esports efforts. Nanzer's role evolved as Fortnite competitive faced challenges (declining viewership, competitive integrity concerns).
- Nanzer has been a prominent voice on esports panels, discussing league structure, player welfare, and the business of competitive gaming.

## Key contributions

- **Overwatch League structure:** Pioneered the city-based franchise model for esports, with teams paying $20M+ entry fees. Teams included ownership from traditional sports (Kraft Group, Comcast, Kroenke Sports).
- **Player benefits:** OWL was one of the first esports leagues to provide guaranteed salaries, health insurance, retirement benefits, and housing for players — formalizing esports labor conditions.
- **Fortnite competitive:** At Epic, Nanzer managed a different model — open-entry tournaments with massive prize pools ($30M for the 2019 World Cup) rather than franchise-based leagues.

## Labour context

- OWL's formalization of player contracts was a milestone for esports labor: minimum salaries ($50k/year initially), team-provided housing, health insurance, and a players' association.
- However, the OWL model proved economically unsustainable. By 2024, most teams had left or the league effectively dissolved. Players and staff were laid off.
- The collapse of OWL (and the Call of Duty League) is cited as evidence that the franchised esports model could not achieve sustainability.
- At Epic, competitive Fortnite has a less structured labor model — players are independent contractors, not league employees.
- No union presence across either OWL, Fortnite competitive, or Epic's esports division.
